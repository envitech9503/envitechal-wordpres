Karachi does not have one water supply. It has at least four, often in the same building: piped KW&SC water in the overhead tank, borewell water pumped from a sub-soil well, tanker water delivered when the pipe runs dry, and RO water from a plant in the basement or a filtration shop on the corner. Each comes with its own problems, and the test that matters for one is close to useless for another. This guide, written from the bench of our water testing lab in Karachi, explains what to test for each source, what the results are compared against, and how to read the two numbers that Karachi households ask about most: TDS and bacteria.

Key takeaways

  • Borewell water in Karachi is usually a salinity and hardness problem, sometimes a nitrate or arsenic problem. Test TDS, hardness, chloride, nitrate, fluoride and arsenic before deciding how to treat it.
  • Tanker water is a bacteriology problem first. Its source changes from delivery to delivery. Test total coliform and E. coli on every new supply, and TDS to detect when a supplier has switched from sweet to brackish water.
  • RO water is a verification problem. Test TDS and bacteria at the outlet to confirm the plant is doing what it claims, and check that TDS has not been driven so low that the water is flat and corrosive to pipework.
  • Piped water in the overhead tank is a storage problem. Residual chlorine and bacteriology tell you whether the tank, not the supply, is the issue.
  • Results are compared with the Pakistan Environmental Quality Standards for drinking water (PEQS) and, where asked, the WHO guidelines. The report shows the limit beside each result.

Borewell water: salinity, hardness and the two hidden parameters

Karachi's sub-soil water sits close to the sea and under decades of urban drainage. The result, across most of the city from DHA and Clifton through Gulshan to North Karachi, is water that is brackish, hard, or both. Total dissolved solids in borewell samples that reach our laboratory commonly run from 1,500 to 5,000 mg/L against a PEQS limit of 1,000 mg/L, and hardness from 500 to over 1,500 mg/L as calcium carbonate against a limit of 500. Chloride follows TDS. None of these three is a direct health hazard at these levels, but they make the water unpalatable, scale geysers and kettles, and ruin RO membranes faster than the supplier's warranty expects.

Two parameters are hidden because the water does not taste of them. Nitrate, from leaking sewers and septic systems, matters most for infants and is limited to 50 mg/L. Arsenic, from the geology in parts of Sindh, has a PEQS limit of 0.05 mg/L and a WHO guideline value of 0.01 mg/L, and it is tasteless, odourless and invisible. Neither can be inferred from TDS. Both are cheap to add to a borewell panel and expensive to discover late.

A sensible first borewell panel in Karachi is therefore: pH, TDS, conductivity, total hardness, calcium, magnesium, chloride, sulphate, nitrate, fluoride, arsenic, iron, and total coliform with E. coli. The chemistry tells you what treatment is needed; the bacteriology tells you whether the well head is sealed against surface contamination.

Tanker water: test the delivery, not the brand

A water tanker is a container, not a source. The same contractor may fill from a licensed hydrant one day and from an unlicensed borewell or an open reservoir the next. Two things follow. First, a single test of tanker water tells you about that delivery and nothing else. Second, the parameters that change between deliveries are the ones worth tracking: TDS, which jumps when the fill point switches from sweet to brackish, and bacteriology, which depends on the fill point, the tanker's own hygiene and the time in transit.

For a household or a small office, the practical routine is a bacteriology test (total coliform and E. coli, limit zero per 100 mL for both) on each new supplier and after any change in taste or smell, with TDS measured at the same time as a cheap fingerprint of the source. For a hotel, hospital, school or food business taking regular tanker deliveries, a monthly sample from the receiving tank, plus residual chlorine checks after dosing, is the pattern Sindh EPA and most auditors expect to see. RO water: confirm the plant, then watch the TDS floor

Reverse osmosis removes most dissolved solids and, when the membranes and the pre-filters are intact, most bacteria. Whether a particular plant is doing so on a particular day is a separate question, and it is the one a test answers. At the outlet, TDS should be a fraction of the feed. A plant fed with 3,000 mg/L borewell water and producing 150 to 300 mg/L is working. The same plant producing 900 mg/L has a damaged membrane, a bypass or a blown seal. Bacteriology at the outlet should be clean; if it is not, the storage tank after the membrane or the dispensing tap is usually the cause, and the fix is cleaning and chlorination, not a new membrane.

There is a lower limit too. RO water driven below roughly 50 mg/L TDS tastes flat and is slightly corrosive to copper and galvanised fittings. Some plants add a remineralisation stage for this reason. A test every three months, or after any servicing, is enough to confirm both the ceiling and the floor. For a filtration shop selling by the can, the same test at the dispensing point is the evidence customers and inspectors ask for.

Piped water in the tank: the chlorine question

Treated piped water leaves the works with a chlorine residual. By the time it has sat in an underground sump and an overhead tank for a week in a Karachi summer, that residual is often gone, and anything that entered the tank has had time to grow. Residual chlorine, measured at the tap, tells you whether the disinfection has survived the journey; PEQS expects 0.2 to 0.5 mg/L at the consumer end. Total coliform and E. coli tell you whether anything has grown. When chlorine is absent and coliforms are present, the answer is tank cleaning and dosing. When chlorine is present and coliforms are still found, the contamination is downstream and the plumbing needs attention.

How to take the sample

For chemistry, run the tap or pump for two minutes, then fill a clean one-litre plastic bottle to the shoulder. For bacteriology, use the sterile bottle the laboratory supplies, do not rinse it, do not touch the inside of the cap, and leave an air gap. If the water is chlorinated the bottle will already contain sodium thiosulphate to stop the chlorine acting in transit. Keep bacteriology samples cool and bring them to the laboratory within 24 hours; within six hours is better. Sample borewells at the pump outlet, tankers from the delivery hose as it fills, RO plants at the dispensing tap, and piped water at the kitchen tap. Our laboratory in Bahadurabad accepts samples Monday to Saturday, 09:00 to 17:00, and the field team can sample on site anywhere in Karachi, which is the right choice when the report is going to a regulator or an auditor. Reading the result

Each line of the report shows the result, the unit, the method, the detection limit and the PEQS limit, with the accreditation mark where the parameter is within the laboratory's PNAC scope (LAB-285). A borewell at 2,800 mg/L TDS and 900 mg/L hardness with clean bacteriology needs softening or RO, not disinfection. A tanker at 400 mg/L TDS with E. coli present needs a different supplier or chlorination, not a filter. An RO outlet at 180 mg/L with clean bacteriology needs nothing until the next quarterly check. Frequently asked questions

Envi Tech AL's PNAC accredited laboratory (LAB-285) in Bahadurabad tests borewell, tanker, RO and piped water, with results compared against PEQS and WHO limits. Samples can be dropped off or collected by the field team.

What should I test tanker water for?

Total coliform and E. coli on each new supplier, plus TDS as a check on the fill source. For businesses receiving regular deliveries, a monthly sample from the receiving tank and residual chlorine after dosing.

What TDS should RO water have?

Well below the PEQS limit of 1,000 mg/L and a small fraction of the feed water; 100 to 300 mg/L is typical for a healthy plant on Karachi borewell feed. Below about 50 mg/L the water is flat and mildly corrosive, which is why some plants remineralise.

Is high TDS in Karachi water dangerous?

TDS itself is a palatability and scaling issue rather than a health hazard at the levels seen in Karachi. The health questions in the same water are nitrate, arsenic and bacteria, which TDS does not reveal and which must be tested separately.

How often should a household test its water?

A borewell once a year and after any change in taste; tanker water on each new supplier; an RO plant every three months and after servicing; piped water in the tank after each tank cleaning. Businesses under Sindh EPA or audit requirements follow the frequency in their monitoring plan.
